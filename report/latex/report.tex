\section{Overview and Environment}

We designed a 4-bit $\times$ 4-bit unsigned array multiplier in Verilog, verified it with an exhaustive self-checking testbench, synthesised it to SKY130 standard cells with Yosys, analysed its timing with OpenSTA, and took it through place and route to a GDSII layout with LibreLane. The design registers its inputs and its output, so every timing path in the core runs from one flip-flop to another. The latency is 2 clock cycles and the throughput is one product per cycle.

\section{Part A: RTL Design and Verification}

\subsection{Full adder}

\texttt{rtl/full\_adder.v} defines module \texttt{full\_adder} with inputs \texttt{a}, \texttt{b}, \texttt{cin} and outputs \texttt{sum}, \texttt{cout}, written only with continuous \texttt{assign} statements:
\begin{align*}
\mathit{sum}  &= a \oplus b \oplus c_{in} && \text{(1 when an odd number of inputs are 1)}\\
\mathit{cout} &= a\,b + a\,c_{in} + b\,c_{in} && \text{(majority: 1 when two or more inputs are 1)}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Array multiplier architecture}

For every bit $b_i$ of B there is one partial product $pp_i = A \wedge b_i$, so $pp_i[j] = a[j]\cdot b[i]$. That gives $4 \times 4 = \mathbf{16}$ \textbf{AND gates}. Row 0 needs no adders. Each of rows 1 to 3 is a 4-bit ripple-carry adder made from 4 full adders, giving $3 \times 4 = \mathbf{12}$ \textbf{full adders}. Each row adds its partial product to the 4 bits passed down from the row above. The lowest sum bit of each row is a finished product bit. Table~\ref{tab:conn} gives the exact connections.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{c>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.2cm}L}
\toprule
\textbf{Row} & \textbf{Inputs to full adder $j$ ($j = 0\ldots3$)} & \textbf{Outputs} \\
\midrule
0 & no adders (AND gates only) & $p[0] = pp_0[0]$; passes down $r_0 = \{0,\, pp_0[3],\, pp_0[2],\, pp_0[1]\}$ \\
1 & $pp_1[j]$, $r_0[j]$, $c_1[j]$ \quad ($c_1[0] = 0$) & sum $s_1[j]$, carry $c_1[j{+}1]$; $p[1] = s_1[0]$; $r_1 = \{c_1[4], s_1[3], s_1[2], s_1[1]\}$ \\
2 & $pp_2[j]$, $r_1[j]$, $c_2[j]$ \quad ($c_2[0] = 0$) & sum $s_2[j]$, carry $c_2[j{+}1]$; $p[2] = s_2[0]$; $r_2 = \{c_2[4], s_2[3], s_2[2], s_2[1]\}$ \\
3 & $pp_3[j]$, $r_2[j]$, $c_3[j]$ \quad ($c_3[0] = 0$) & sum $s_3[j]$, carry $c_3[j{+}1]$; $p[3] = s_3[0]$; $p[7{:}4] = \{c_3[4], s_3[3], s_3[2], s_3[1]\}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Connections of the array (from Section 2 of the assignment).}
\label{tab:conn}
\end{table}

\textbf{Implementation in \texttt{rtl/mult\_array.v}:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ports:} \texttt{clk}, \texttt{rst\_n} (asynchronous, active low), \texttt{a[3:0]}, \texttt{b[3:0]}, \texttt{p[7:0]} (registered). These names match the specification exactly.
  \item \textbf{Input registers:} \texttt{a\_r} and \texttt{b\_r} (8 flip-flops) capture \texttt{a} and \texttt{b} on every rising edge. Reset clears them to 0.
  \item \textbf{Partial products:} \texttt{pp$i$ = a\_r \& \{4\{b\_r[$i$]\}\}}, which gives the 16 AND gates.
  \item \textbf{Adder rows:} three \texttt{generate} loops, each with 4 \texttt{full\_adder} instances (\texttt{row1.fa}, \texttt{row2.fa}, \texttt{row3.fa}), so 12 instances in total. The carry chains \texttt{c1}, \texttt{c2}, \texttt{c3} are 5 bits wide, with bit 0 tied to 0.
  \item \textbf{Output register:} \texttt{prod = \{c3[4], s3[3:0], s2[0], s1[0], pp0[0]\}} is registered into \texttt{p} (8 flip-flops). That makes \textbf{16 flip-flops} in total.
  \item The \texttt{*} operator is not used anywhere in the RTL.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Error-injection test (task 6).} To check that the testbench really catches errors, we temporarily changed the sum in \texttt{full\_adder.v} from \texttt{a \^{} b \^{} cin} to \texttt{a \^{} b}, which ignores the carry-in. We re-ran the testbench and then restored the file:

\begin{lstlisting}
MISMATCH: a=3 b=3 expected=9 got=5
MISMATCH: a=3 b=6 expected=18 got=10
MISMATCH: a=3 b=7 expected=21 got=9
MISMATCH: a=3 b=11 expected=33 got=29
MISMATCH: a=3 b=12 expected=36 got=20
... (90 more MISMATCH lines)
FAIL: 256 vectors checked, 95 errors
\end{lstlisting}

95 of the 256 products are wrong. A vector can only fail when some full adder receives a carry-in of 1. Many vectors never produce an internal carry, for example any product with $a = 0$, $b = 0$, or an operand that is a power of two, so 161 vectors still pass. So the testbench does detect a broken adder. The change was undone (\texttt{git checkout rtl/full\_adder.v}) and the testbench prints PASS again.

\subsection{Hand-worked example (task 5)}

A = last digit of a roll number + 5. We used roll number 2023112014, whose last digit is 4, so $\mathbf{A = 9 = 1001_2}$. $\mathbf{B = 7 = 0111_2}$ (A $\neq$ 7, so B stays 7). The expected product is $9 \times 7 = \mathbf{63 = 0011\,1111_2 = \text{0x3F}}$.

\needspace{15\baselineskip}
\textbf{Shift-and-add layout (as in Section 2 of the assignment):}
\begin{lstlisting}
            1 0 0 1     A = 9
          x 0 1 1 1     B = 7
    ---------------
            1 0 0 1     pp0 = A AND b0 (b0 = 1)
          1 0 0 1       pp1 = A AND b1 (b1 = 1), shifted 1
        1 0 0 1         pp2 = A AND b2 (b2 = 1), shifted 2
      0 0 0 0           pp3 = A AND b3 (b3 = 0), shifted 3
    ---------------
    0 0 1 1 1 1 1 1     P = 63
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{The same product through the adder rows (Table~\ref{tab:conn}):}
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{ccccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Row} & $\mathbf{pp_i}$ & $\mathbf{+\,r_{i-1}}$ & $\mathbf{=\{c,\,s\}}$ & \textbf{Outputs} \\
\midrule
0 & \texttt{1001} & -- & -- & $p[0]=1$, $r_0=$ \texttt{0100} \\
1 & \texttt{1001} & \texttt{0100} & \texttt{0 1101} & $p[1]=1$, $r_1=$ \texttt{0110} \\
2 & \texttt{1001} & \texttt{0110} & \texttt{0 1111} & $p[2]=1$, $r_2=$ \texttt{0111} \\
3 & \texttt{0000} & \texttt{0111} & \texttt{0 0111} & $p[3]=1$, $p[7{:}4]=$ \texttt{0011} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Row-by-row evaluation of $9 \times 7$ in the array.}
\end{table}

$P = \{p[7{:}4], p[3], p[2], p[1], p[0]\} = \{0011, 1, 1, 1, 1\} = 0011\,1111_2 = 63$, the same as the shift-and-add result. No full adder produces a carry for this input pair.

\subsection{Finding the maximum frequency}

\begin{lstlisting}
STA_PERIOD=10.0 sta -exit sta/sta.tcl   -> worst slack max 7.40  (report: +7.396 ns)
T_min = T - WNS = 10.000 - 7.396 = 2.604 ns
F_max = 1000 / T_min = 1000 / 2.604 = 384.02 MHz
STA_PERIOD=2.60 sta -exit sta/sta.tcl   -> worst slack max -0.00 (report: -0.004 ns)
\end{lstlisting}

At $T = 2.60$ ns (\Tmin{} rounded to 0.01 ns) the slack is $-0.004$ ns, which is effectively zero. The tiny violation comes only from rounding 2.604 ns down to 2.60 ns. This confirms \Tmin.

\section{Answers to the Report Questions}

\question{Q1. The array needs 16 AND gates and 12 full adders. How many cells did synthesis produce for the logic? Explain why the number of cells is not simply 16 + 12.}

Synthesis produced 63 cells, of which 16 are flip-flops, so the logic uses \textbf{47 cells} (340.3\umsq) rather than 28. A ``full adder'' and an ``AND gate'' are RTL building blocks, not one library cell each. With \texttt{-flatten}, Yosys removed the module boundaries, and ABC re-implemented the whole Boolean function with whatever SKY130 cells gave a good area/delay result:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One full adder needs at least two cells.} Each full adder becomes a sum cell (\texttt{xor2}, \texttt{xnor2}, \texttt{xor3} or \texttt{xnor3}; 10 in total) plus a carry cell (\texttt{maj3}) or an equivalent NAND/AOI network. 12 full adders alone would therefore need about 24 cells or more.
  \item \textbf{AND gates are merged into other logic.} Many partial-product ANDs are absorbed into compound gates such as \texttt{a21oi}, \texttt{a22oi}, \texttt{o311ai}, \texttt{o2111ai}, \texttt{nand3} and \texttt{nand4}. Only a few remain as plain \texttt{and2/3/4} cells.
  \item \textbf{Constant inputs simplify some adders.} The carry into every row ($c_i[0] = 0$) and the top bit of $r_0$ are constant 0. Those full adders reduce to half adders, built from an \texttt{xor2} plus a \texttt{nand2} or \texttt{and2}.
  \item \textbf{Inversions need cells.} NAND/NOR/AOI cells are inverting, and a few signals need \texttt{clkinv}, \texttt{nand2b} or \texttt{nor2b}.
\end{itemize}
The cell count measures how the logic maps onto the library, not how the RTL was written.

\question{Q2. What is the critical path of your design, from which register to which register? How many cells are on it, and how does this compare with the estimate of about 2N full-adder delays?}

The critical path starts at input register \texttt{a\_r[1]} (\texttt{\_095\_}, \texttt{dfrtp\_1}) and ends at output register \texttt{p[6]} (\texttt{\_092\_}, \texttt{dfrtp\_1}). It has \textbf{10 cells}: the launching flip-flop, \textbf{8 combinational cells} (\texttt{clkinv} $\to$ \texttt{o311ai} $\to$ \texttt{a21oi} $\to$ \texttt{maj3} $\to$ \texttt{xnor2} $\to$ \texttt{maj3} $\to$ \texttt{maj3} $\to$ \texttt{xnor3}), and the capturing flip-flop. The arrival time is 2.471 ns, giving $T_{\min} = 2.604$ ns. The path follows the carry down through the adder rows into product bit 6, the longest ripple in the array. The estimate for $N = 4$ is $2N = 8$ full-adder delays, which matches the 8 combinational cells well. A full-adder carry maps to a single \texttt{maj3} cell, so one full-adder delay is about one cell delay here, around 0.26 ns on average.

\question{Q3. What percentage of the total cell area is flip-flops? Why is this percentage large for such a small design?}

Flip-flops take \textbf{400.38 of 740.71\umsq{} = 54.05\,\%} of the cell area, although they are only 16 of the 63 cells (25\,\%). Each \texttt{dfrtp\_1} is 25.02\umsq. That is 6.7 times a \texttt{nand2\_1} (3.75\umsq) and about 3.5 times the average logic cell (7.2\umsq). An edge-triggered flip-flop with asynchronous reset is two latches plus clock buffering and reset logic, which is a few dozen transistors compared with 4 for a NAND2. The other reason is scaling. The array logic grows as $N^2$ (16 ANDs and 12 adders), while the registers grow only as $4N$ (16 flip-flops). At $N = 4$ the logic is still small, so the registers dominate. For an 8-bit multiplier the flip-flop share would fall to roughly 35\,\% (see Q6).

\question{Q4. Why is the post-route $F_{\max}$ lower than the $F_{\max}$ from Part C? Which kinds of cells did the flow add, and why is each kind needed?}

The post-route \Fmax{} is \textbf{131.58 MHz}, against \textbf{384.02 MHz} from Part C, about 2.9 times lower. The two numbers measure different things:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Corner.} Part C used only the typical library (tt, 1.80 V, 25\,$^\circ$C). LibreLane signs off at 9 corners and reports the worst, which is the slow corner ss, 1.60 V, 100\,$^\circ$C with maximum wire RC. In the final run the typical corner alone allows $1000/(8.0 - 4.240) \approx$ \textbf{266 MHz}, while the slow corner gives 131.6 MHz. The slow corner accounts for about a factor of 2.
  \item \textbf{Real wires.} Part C had no parasitics. After routing, 2499\um{} of wire and 561 vias add extracted resistance and capacitance to every net. The pins are on the edge of a 150\um{} die, so some nets are long.
  \item \textbf{Real clock tree.} Part C used an ideal clock. After clock-tree synthesis there is insertion delay and about 0.26 to 0.28 ns of skew between flip-flops, which reduces the time available for setup.
  \item \textbf{Different netlist.} LibreLane synthesises the RTL again with its own script and then inserts buffers, so the post-route critical path is not the same 8-cell path analysed in Part C.
\end{enumerate}
The flow added these cells (Table~\ref{tab:cells}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Timing-repair buffers (21):} \texttt{buf\_2}, \texttt{clkdlybuf4s*}. They fix slew, capacitance and fanout limits on long nets.
  \item \textbf{Clock buffers (3):} \texttt{clkbuf\_16}. They distribute the clock to 16 flip-flops with low skew.
  \item \textbf{Tap cells (245):} they bias the wells and substrate to prevent latch-up.
  \item \textbf{Decap cells (1298):} on-chip supply decoupling against switching noise.
  \item \textbf{Filler cells (254):} keep the wells, implants and power rails continuous across row gaps, so the layout passes DRC.
\end{itemize}

\question{Q5. What does a DRC check verify, and what does an LVS check verify? Why must both pass before a chip can be manufactured?}

\textbf{DRC (design rule check)} verifies the geometry of the layout against the foundry's rules for every layer. It checks minimum widths, spacings, enclosures, overlaps, via rules, density and antenna ratios. These rules encode what the fab can print reliably. A violation can cause shorts, opens or very low yield.

\textbf{LVS (layout versus schematic)} extracts the transistors and their connections from the layout and compares them with the intended netlist: same devices, same nets, same pins. It confirms that the layout is the circuit we designed, with no missing connection, accidental short or swapped pin.

Each check covers what the other cannot. A layout can be perfectly manufacturable (DRC clean) and still implement the wrong circuit, and a layout can be logically correct (LVS clean) but impossible to fabricate. A mask set is very expensive and cannot be fixed after tape-out, so both must pass first. Our final run has \textbf{0 DRC errors} in Magic and KLayout (the router's 6 first-iteration violations were fixed in its second iteration) and \textbf{0 LVS errors} in Netgen. It also has 0 antenna violations.

\question{Q6. Using the formulas in Section 2, estimate how the number of full adders and the critical path delay would change if the multiplier were 8 bits wide instead of 4. Show your working.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{$N = 4$ (ours)} & \textbf{$N = 8$ (estimate)} & \textbf{Change} \\
\midrule
AND gates & $N^2$ & 16 & 64 & $\times 4$ \\

\bottomrule

\textbf{Working.} In our Part C result the 8 full-adder delays take 2.073 ns of logic, so one full-adder delay is about $2.073/8 \approx 0.26$ ns. The flip-flop overhead is $0.398$ (CLK$\to$Q) $+ 0.134$ (setup) $= 0.532$ ns. For $N = 8$:
\begin{align*}
T_{\min}(8\text{-bit}) &\approx 0.532 + 16 \times 0.259 = 0.532 + 4.146 \approx 4.68 \text{ ns}\\
F_{\max}(8\text{-bit}) &\approx 1000 / 4.68 \approx 214 \text{ MHz} \quad \text{(vs 384 MHz for 4 bits; typical corner, pre-layout)}
\end{align*}

So the number of full adders grows about 4.7 times (quadratically in $N$), while the critical path delay grows only about 2 times in its logic part (linearly in $N$). \Tmin{} rises from 2.60 ns to about 4.7 ns, 1.8 times, because the flip-flop overhead stays the same. For area, if the logic scales with the gate count, $(56 + 64)/(12 + 16) \approx 4.3$ times 340\umsq, it would be about 1460\umsq. With 32 flip-flops at about 800\umsq{} the total is about 2260\umsq, and the flip-flop share falls to about 35\,\%.
